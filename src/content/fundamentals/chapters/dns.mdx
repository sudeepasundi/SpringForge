## Why it matters

Computers connect to IP addresses; people and configuration files use names like
`payments.internal` or `api.example.com`. **DNS** (Domain Name System) turns one into the other. When it
misbehaves, everything looks broken at once — which is why "it's always DNS" is an operations joke.

## How it works

### Resolution, step by step

1. The application asks the operating system, which first checks `/etc/hosts` and its own cache.
2. The OS asks a **recursive resolver** (your ISP's, your cloud's, or a public one such as 1.1.1.1).
3. The resolver asks, in turn, a **root** server, the **TLD** servers for `.com`, and the
   **authoritative** servers for `example.com`.
4. The answer travels back and is **cached at every step** for its TTL.

Most lookups never go past step 2 — the answer is already cached.

### TTL and caching

Every answer carries a **TTL** (time to live) in seconds. Resolvers reuse the answer until it expires.

- A **long TTL** (hours) means fewer lookups but slow changes — after you move a server, some clients
  keep the old address until their cache expires.
- A **short TTL** (seconds to minutes) makes changes and failover quick at the cost of more lookups.
- Before a planned migration, **lower the TTL in advance**, wait for the old TTL to pass, then switch.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Applications cache DNS too">
The JVM caches successful lookups — for 30 seconds by default in current JDKs, controlled by the
`networkaddress.cache.ttl` security property (older setups with a security manager cached them
forever). Connection pools also keep connections to old addresses. After a DNS-based failover, a service may keep talking to the
old IP until connections are recycled.
</Callout>

### DNS inside clusters and clouds

- **Kubernetes** gives each Service a name: `orders.shop.svc.cluster.local` resolves to a stable
  virtual IP. Short names (`orders`) work within the namespace thanks to **search domains**.
- **Private DNS zones** in the cloud resolve internal names such as a database endpoint.
- **DNS-based load balancing** returns several A records (or different ones per region) — simple, but
  clients may cache and ignore changes.

## In a Spring and Java app

- Services find each other by DNS names in Kubernetes, or through a registry such as Eureka —
  compared in [Service Discovery](/learn/microservices-fundamentals/service-discovery).
- Configure database and service URLs by **name**, never by IP.
