## Why it matters

A user uploads a video. Transcoding takes three minutes. Making them wait on the HTTP request means
timeouts and a tied-up server. Sending a welcome email should not fail a registration if the email
provider is down. Putting work on a **queue** solves both — and introduces new questions about ordering,
duplicates and failures.

## How it works

### The idea

The producer puts a message on a queue and moves on; a consumer takes it off and does the work later.

What you gain:

- **Responsiveness** — the user gets an answer immediately.
- **Decoupling** — the producer does not need the consumer to be up, or even to know who it is.
- **Load levelling** — a burst fills the queue; workers drain it at a steady rate instead of the database melting.
- **Independent scaling** — add workers when the queue grows.
- **Retries** — a failed job can be retried without the user noticing.

**Kafka** combines both: a topic is a durable, ordered log; each **consumer group** receives every
message (pub/sub), and within a group, partitions are shared among instances (work distribution).

### Commands and events

- A **command** asks for something to happen: `SendInvoice`. One handler; it may fail.
- An **event** states that something happened: `OrderPlaced`. Any number of subscribers; the publisher
  does not care what they do.

### Acknowledgements and delivery

A consumer **acknowledges** a message after processing it. If it crashes first, the message is
redelivered. So consumers see messages **at least once** and must be idempotent
([previous chapter](/fundamentals/consistency-cap-idempotency)).

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Acknowledge after the work, not before">
Acknowledging on receipt and then crashing loses the message. Acknowledging after the work may process it
twice. Choose the second, and make processing idempotent.
</Callout>

### Failures, retries and dead letters

<Steps>
  <Step title="Retry transient failures">

With backoff: the database blipped, the API returned 503.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Stop retrying permanent failures">

A malformed message will fail forever. Endless retries block the queue (a **poison message**).

  </Step>
  <Step title="Move it to a dead-letter queue">

After N attempts, park the message in a **DLQ** with the error, alert, and let a person inspect,
fix and replay it.

  </Step>
</Steps>

### Ordering

Strict global ordering and parallel processing do not mix. Most systems guarantee order only within a
**partition** or **message group**: all events for order 42 share a key, so they land in the same
partition and are processed in sequence, while different orders proceed in parallel.

### Back-pressure

If producers outpace consumers, the queue grows without bound. Watch **queue depth** and **consumer lag**;
scale consumers, slow producers, or shed load. A queue hides overload for a while — it does not remove it.

### When not to use a queue

- The user needs the result **now** (a price check, a login) — call synchronously.
- You need strong consistency with the caller's transaction — or use the outbox pattern to publish reliably.
- The added moving parts are not justified for a small, simple app — a database table polled by a
  scheduled job is a perfectly good queue at low volume.

## When you'll meet it

- **Sending emails, generating PDFs, resizing images** — background jobs.
- **"The same email was sent three times"** — redelivery with a non-idempotent consumer.
- **Consumer lag growing all afternoon** — consumers too slow or stuck on a poison message.
- **Events processed out of order** — wrong partition key.
- **Interview:** "Queue versus topic?", "How do you handle failed messages?", "How do you guarantee order?"
