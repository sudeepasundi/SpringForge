## Why it matters

Normalised data lives in many tables; almost every useful question needs several of them at once.
Joins are also where the subtle bugs are: rows that vanish because of an inner join, totals that double
because of a one-to-many join, and "customers with no orders" queries that return the wrong people.

## How it works

A join combines rows from two tables where a condition matches — usually a foreign key equal to a
primary key.

### INNER JOIN — only matches

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  query={`SELECT c.name, o.id AS order_id, o.status
FROM customer c
JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id     -- JOIN means INNER JOIN
ORDER BY c.name, o.id;`}
/>

Customers with no orders do not appear at all.

### LEFT JOIN — keep every row on the left

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  query={`SELECT c.name, o.id AS order_id
FROM customer c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
ORDER BY o.id IS NULL DESC, c.name;`}
/>

Grace Kim and Nora Svensson now appear, with `NULL` order columns.

### Anti-join: rows with no match

"Customers who never ordered" is a left join keeping only the rows where the right side is missing —
or `NOT EXISTS`:

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  query={`SELECT c.name
FROM customer c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE o.id IS NULL;

SELECT c.name
FROM customer c
WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.id);`}
/>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A WHERE on the right table turns a LEFT JOIN into an INNER JOIN">
`LEFT JOIN orders o … WHERE o.status = 'PAID'` removes the unmatched rows again, because their
`o.status` is `NULL`. To keep them, put the condition in the join: `LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id
= c.id AND o.status = 'PAID'`.
</Callout>

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  title="Condition in ON versus WHERE"
  query={`-- every customer, with their PAID orders if any
SELECT c.name, o.id AS paid_order
FROM customer c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id AND o.status = 'PAID'
ORDER BY c.name;`}
/>

### RIGHT and FULL joins

`RIGHT JOIN` is a `LEFT JOIN` with the tables swapped — most people rewrite it as a left join. `FULL JOIN`
keeps unmatched rows from both sides. SQLite (3.39+) and PostgreSQL support both; **MySQL has no FULL
JOIN** — emulate it with a `LEFT JOIN … UNION … RIGHT JOIN`.

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="hr"
  title="Departments and employees, including a department with nobody and people with no department"
  query={`SELECT d.name AS department, e.first_name
FROM department d
FULL JOIN employee e ON e.department_id = d.id
WHERE d.id IS NULL OR e.id IS NULL;`}
/>

### Joining many tables

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  query={`SELECT o.id AS order_id, c.name AS customer, p.name AS product, oi.quantity
FROM orders o
JOIN customer c    ON c.id = o.customer_id
JOIN order_item oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
JOIN product p     ON p.id = oi.product_id
WHERE o.id <= 4
ORDER BY o.id, p.name;`}
/>

### Self-join: a table joined to itself

Each employee row has a `manager_id` pointing at another employee row. Join the table to itself with
two aliases:

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="hr"
  query={`SELECT e.first_name AS employee,
       m.first_name AS manager
FROM employee e
LEFT JOIN employee m ON m.id = e.manager_id
ORDER BY e.id;`}
/>

### CROSS JOIN: every combination

`CROSS JOIN` pairs every row with every row — 16 products × 5 departments is 80 rows. Useful for
generating combinations (every product for every store); dangerous when it happens by accident, such as
a missing join condition.

### Fan-out: the join that doubles your totals

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Summing after joining two one-to-many tables">

```sql
-- items AND reviews per product: each item row is repeated once per review
SELECT p.name, SUM(oi.quantity) AS units, COUNT(r.id) AS reviews
FROM product p
JOIN order_item oi ON oi.product_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN review r ON r.product_id = p.id
GROUP BY p.name;
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="Aggregate each side first, then join">

```sql
SELECT p.name, s.units, COALESCE(rv.reviews, 0) AS reviews
FROM product p
JOIN (SELECT product_id, SUM(quantity) AS units FROM order_item GROUP BY product_id) s
  ON s.product_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN (SELECT product_id, COUNT(*) AS reviews FROM review GROUP BY product_id) rv
  ON rv.product_id = p.id;
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

<SqlPlayground
  dataset="shop"
  title="See the fan-out: compare the units column in both results"
  query={`SELECT p.name, SUM(oi.quantity) AS units, COUNT(r.id) AS reviews
FROM product p
JOIN order_item oi ON oi.product_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN review r ON r.product_id = p.id
WHERE p.id IN (1, 7, 10)
GROUP BY p.name;

SELECT p.name, s.units, COALESCE(rv.reviews, 0) AS reviews
FROM product p
JOIN (SELECT product_id, SUM(quantity) AS units FROM order_item GROUP BY product_id) s
  ON s.product_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN (SELECT product_id, COUNT(*) AS reviews FROM review GROUP BY product_id) rv
  ON rv.product_id = p.id
WHERE p.id IN (1, 7, 10);`}
/>

## Exercises

<SqlExercise
  dataset="hr"
  prompt="List each employee's first_name with their department's name (as department). Include employees without a department."
  solution={`SELECT e.first_name, d.name AS department
FROM employee e
LEFT JOIN department d ON d.id = e.department_id;`}
  hint="LEFT JOIN from employee to department."
/>

<SqlExercise
  dataset="shop"
  prompt="Find products that have never been ordered. Return their name."
  solution={`SELECT p.name
FROM product p
LEFT JOIN order_item oi ON oi.product_id = p.id
WHERE oi.product_id IS NULL;`}
  hint="An anti-join: LEFT JOIN then keep rows where the right side is NULL (or use NOT EXISTS)."
/>

<SqlExercise
  dataset="hr"
  prompt="Find employees who earn more than their manager. Return the employee's first_name, their salary, and the manager's first_name as manager."
  solution={`SELECT e.first_name, e.salary, m.first_name AS manager
FROM employee e
JOIN employee m ON m.id = e.manager_id
WHERE e.salary > m.salary;`}
  hint="Self-join employee to employee on manager_id, then compare salaries."
/>

<SqlExercise
  dataset="shop"
  prompt="For each customer who has placed orders, return their name and the total amount spent on non-cancelled orders (quantity times unit_price), rounded to 2 decimals, as spent."
  solution={`SELECT c.name, ROUND(SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price), 2) AS spent
FROM customer c
JOIN orders o      ON o.customer_id = c.id
JOIN order_item oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.status <> 'CANCELLED'
GROUP BY c.id, c.name;`}
  hint="Join three tables, filter out cancelled orders, group by customer."
/>
