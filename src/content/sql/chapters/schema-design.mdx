## Why it matters

A poor schema makes every query harder and every change riskier for the life of the system: the same
fact stored in three places drifts apart, a comma-separated column cannot be joined, and a missing
junction table forces awkward workarounds. Normalisation is a short set of rules that avoid these
problems.

## How it works

### The problem: one wide table

```text
order_id | customer_name | customer_email   | products                    | category_names
---------+---------------+------------------+-----------------------------+----------------------
1        | Aisha Patel   | aisha@mail.test  | Modern Java, Dune, Dune     | Programming, Fiction
4        | Aisha Patel   | aisha@mail.test  | Learning SQL, The Hobbit, … | Programming, Fiction, …
```

- **Update anomaly**: Aisha changes her email — every order row must be updated, and one will be missed.
- **Insert anomaly**: you cannot record a product that has never been ordered.
- **Delete anomaly**: deleting her only order deletes the only record of the customer.
- **Lists in a column** cannot be joined, counted or constrained.

### First normal form (1NF): one value per cell

No lists or repeating groups; each row identified by a key.

<Compare>
  <Bad title="A list in a column">

```text
order_id | products
1        | Modern Java, Dune
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="One row per item">

```text
order_id | product_id | quantity
1        | 1          | 1
1        | 4          | 2
```

That is the shop's `order_item` table.

  </Good>
</Compare>

### Second normal form (2NF): depend on the whole key

In a table with a composite key, every non-key column must depend on **all** of it. In `order_item
(order_id, product_id, quantity, product_name)`, `product_name` depends only on `product_id` — so it
belongs in `product`.

### Third normal form (3NF): depend on nothing but the key

No column should depend on another non-key column. In `employee (id, department_id, department_name)`,
`department_name` depends on `department_id`, not on the employee — so it belongs in `department`.

> Every non-key column must depend on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key.

Junction tables often carry their own data — `role` and `hours` here, `quantity` and `unit_price` in
`order_item`.

### When to denormalise

Normalise first; denormalise deliberately, with a reason and a way to keep copies correct:

- **History**: `order_item.unit_price` copies the price at the time of the order — the product's price
  changes later, but the order must not. (That is correct modelling, not a shortcut.)
- **Read performance**: a cached `order_count` on customer, or a reporting table, updated by the
  application, a trigger or a scheduled job.
- **Analytics**: star schemas and wide fact tables in a data warehouse.

### Keys: natural or surrogate?

- A **natural key** is a real-world identifier (email, ISBN, country code). It can change and may not
  be as unique as it seems.
- A **surrogate key** (an identity or UUID) has no meaning and never changes. Most application tables use
  one as the primary key **and** a `UNIQUE` constraint on the natural key.

### Naming conventions

Pick one and apply it everywhere: singular or plural table names (be consistent), `snake_case`,
`<table>_id` for foreign keys, `created_at`/`updated_at` timestamps, `is_`/`has_` for booleans, and no
reserved words (which is why the shop's table is `orders`, not `order`).

  dataset="hr"
  prompt="Show each project's name and the number of people assigned to it (as people), including projects with nobody (0)."
  solution={`SELECT p.name, COUNT(a.employee_id) AS people
FROM project p
LEFT JOIN assignment a ON a.project_id = p.id
GROUP BY p.id, p.name;`}

<SqlExercise
