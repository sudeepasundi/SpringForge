## Why it matters

Code review catches bugs, spreads knowledge across the team, and keeps a codebase consistent. It is also
where many juniors feel most exposed — both when their code is reviewed and when they are asked to review
a senior's. Doing it well on both sides is a skill.

## Making your pull request easy to review

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Hard to review">

- 1,800 changed lines across 40 files.
- Title: "fixes".
- A refactor, a new feature and a formatting change mixed together.
- No description, no tests, CI red.

</Bad>
  <Good title="Easy to review">

- About 200 lines, one purpose.
- Title: "Apply loyalty discount to large consumer orders".
- A description: the problem, the approach, what to look at closely, how it was tested.
- Refactoring in a separate commit; tests included; CI green.

</Good>
</Compare>

A good description template:

```markdown
## What and why
Loyalty discount was skipped for orders over 500 (ticket SHOP-812). The rule meant to exclude B2B
orders checked the total instead of the customer type.

## How
- `LoyaltyDiscountPolicy` now checks `customer.isBusiness()`.
- Added tests for a large consumer order and a B2B order.

## Review notes
The interesting change is in `LoyaltyDiscountPolicy.java`; the rest is test data.
```

**Review your own diff first.** Reading it in the review tool, as a reviewer would, catches leftover debug
lines, commented-out code and missing tests before anyone else sees them.

## Reviewing someone else's code

Look at things in roughly this order — the most important first:

### Writing comments people are glad to receive

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Vague or harsh">

"This is wrong." · "Why would you do it this way?" · "Use a map." · "??"

</Bad>
  <Good title="Specific, kind and actionable">

"If `items` is empty, `items.get(0)` on line 42 throws — should we return an empty result instead?"

"Suggestion (non-blocking): a `Map<Long, Customer>` built once would avoid the lookup inside the loop,
which is O(n²) for large imports."

"Nice — the builder makes these tests much easier to read."

</Good>
</Compare>

- **Ask questions** rather than make accusations: you may be missing context.
- **Label severity**: blocking, suggestion, nit, question. Reviewers who mark everything as a must slow
  teams down.
- **Explain why**, and link to docs or examples.
- **Praise good things** — it tells people what to keep doing.
- **Review promptly**: a pull request waiting two days blocks a colleague.

As a junior reviewing a senior's code: your questions are valuable. "I didn't understand why this is
cached — could you explain?" either teaches you something or reveals a missing comment.

## Receiving feedback

- The review is about the code, not about you. Everyone's code gets comments.
- **Assume good intent**; ask for clarification when a comment is unclear.
- You may disagree — explain your reasoning, and when it is a matter of taste, it is fine to defer.
- Reply to every comment (a fix, an answer, or "done") so the reviewer can follow along.
- Turn repeated comments into learning: if you get the same note three times, it is a habit to build.
