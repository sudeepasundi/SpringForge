## Why it matters

The most expensive bug is solving the wrong problem. In interviews, candidates lose most points not on
code but on misreading the question. At work, a feature built from a misunderstood ticket costs days. A
few minutes spent pinning the problem down pays back every time.

## How to do it

### 1. Restate it in your own words

If you cannot say it simply, you do not understand it yet. Say it out loud or write it as a comment.

> Ticket: "Customers should get a discount on repeat orders."
>
> Restated: "When a customer places an order and they already have at least one **completed** order,
> apply a discount." — which immediately raises questions: how much? Completed or any status? Per
> order or once?

Sizes matter because they tell you how fast the solution must be — see
[Brute Force First](/craft/brute-force-first).

### 3. Work examples by hand

Pick a normal case, a small case and an awkward case. Solve them **by hand**, slowly, noticing what you
do. Your hand-method is often the algorithm.

## Seeing it on a problem

<Walkthrough
  title="Merge two sorted lists of meeting times"
  problem="You are given two lists of time slots, each sorted by start time. Return a single list of all slots, sorted."
>
  <Stage title="Restate" prompt="Say what is asked in one sentence, and write down anything that seems unclear.">

"Combine two already-sorted lists into one sorted list."

Unclear:

- What is a "slot" — a start and end time? Sorted by start only?
- Should overlapping slots be merged into one, or just both kept?
- If two slots start at the same time, which comes first?

These are exactly the questions to ask an interviewer or a product owner. Suppose the answer is: *keep
every slot, do not merge; ties in any order.*

</Stage>
  <Stage title="Inputs, outputs, constraints" prompt="List the types, the output and the sizes you need to know.">

- Input: two lists of `Slot(start, end)`, each sorted by `start`. Either may be empty.
- Output: a new list with every slot, sorted by `start`.
- Sizes: up to a few thousand each (a calendar), so almost anything is fast enough — but "sorted" hints
  that we should use that fact.

</Stage>
  <Stage title="Examples by hand" prompt="Merge [9:00, 11:00] with [10:00] by hand. What are you doing?">

```text
A: 9:00  11:00
B: 10:00
Compare heads: 9:00 < 10:00 → take 9:00 from A
Compare heads: 11:00 > 10:00 → take 10:00 from B
B is empty → take the rest of A: 11:00
Result: 9:00, 10:00, 11:00
```

You compared the two fronts and took the smaller — the classic **merge** step of merge sort.

</Stage>
  <Stage title="Edge cases" prompt="Which inputs might break a naive implementation?">

- One or both lists empty.
- One list much longer than the other (the leftover tail).
- Equal start times.
- All slots of one list before all of the other.

Writing these down now means the code will handle them by design, not by patching.

</Stage>
</Walkthrough>

## Clarifying questions are a strength

In interviews, asking two or three good questions signals experience. At work, it prevents rework.

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Assuming silently">

Build a discount on *every* repeat order, including cancelled ones, as a percentage — then learn in
review that the business wanted a fixed amount, once, on completed orders only.

</Bad>
  <Good title="Confirming in writing">

"To check I have this right: a 10% discount when the customer has at least one delivered order, applied
to every later order, not stacking with coupons. Correct?" — one message, a day saved.

</Good>
</Compare>
