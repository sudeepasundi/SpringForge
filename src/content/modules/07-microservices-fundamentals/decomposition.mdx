Once you have decided to split, the next question decides whether the result is workable: **where do
the lines go?**

Get it wrong and every feature touches three services, every change needs a coordinated release, and
you have the distributed monolith from the previous lesson. The techniques for getting it right come
from domain-driven design, and the one that matters most is the bounded context.

## The mistake: slicing by technical layer

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Layers as services">

Every feature needs all three. Adding a field to an order means three repositories, three pull
requests, three deployments — coordinated. The network now sits between layers that change together.

```text
api-service          →  all controllers
business-service     →  all business logic
data-service         →  all repositories
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="Capabilities as services">

Each service owns a business capability end to end — its API, its rules, its data. Adding a field to
an order is one repository, one pull request, one deploy.

```text
order-service        →  ordering: API, rules, and the orders it owns
inventory-service    →  stock: API, rules, and the stock levels it owns
payment-service      →  payments: API, rules, and the transactions it owns
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

The heuristic behind this: **things that change together belong together.** A layer is a technical
grouping, and technical groupings almost never match the shape of change.

## Bounded contexts

A bounded context is a boundary within which a term has exactly one meaning. That sounds abstract
until you notice how often a word means different things to different parts of a business.

The practical consequence is the part people resist: **the same real-world entity is modelled
separately in each context**, sharing only an identifier. Billing's `Customer` has a tax id and no
ticket history. Support's has ticket history and no credit limit. Neither is incomplete — each is
complete *for its context*.

Trying to build one canonical `Customer` shared by every service produces a type that every team
must agree on to change. That is the coupling you were splitting to avoid.

## Finding the boundaries

Three techniques, in increasing order of effort and reliability.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Listen for where the language changes">

Sit with the business and note where the same word starts meaning something different, or where a
new word appears. Those shifts are context boundaries. When the warehouse team says "order" and
means something different from what the finance team means, you have found a seam.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Run an event storming session">

Get domain experts and engineers in a room and map the business events in time order — *order
placed*, *payment authorised*, *stock reserved*, *shipment dispatched*. Then group them by who cares
about them. The groupings are candidate contexts, and this surfaces boundaries far faster than
studying the code.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the candidate against change history">

Boundaries are a claim about what changes together, and git already knows. Pull the files changed in
each commit over the last year and look at which proposed services co-occur.

```bash
git log --format=%H --since=1.year -- . | while read sha; do
  git show --pretty=format: --name-only "$sha" | grep -oE '^src/main/java/[a-z]+/[a-z]+' | sort -u | paste -sd,
done | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20
```

If two candidate services appear together in 60% of commits, the boundary between them is wrong.
This is the only one of the three that produces evidence rather than opinion.

  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="The two questions that test a boundary">
**Can this service be changed and deployed without changing another?** If a typical feature spans
two services, the line is in the wrong place.

**Can it answer its own questions?** If serving one request requires three synchronous calls
outward, the data is on the wrong side of the boundary.
</Callout>

## Every service owns its data

This is the rule with the least negotiation in it. **A service owns its database, and no other
service touches it.** Not read-only access, not a shared reporting view, not "just this one join".

<Compare>
  <Bad title="A shared database">

The schema is now a public API with no version and no owner. Inventory cannot add a column without
checking with three teams, and a migration can break a service whose code nobody on the migrating
team has read. You have coupled the services at the tightest possible point.

```text
order-service      ─┐
inventory-service  ─┼──▶  one PostgreSQL, one schema
payment-service    ─┘
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="A database per service">

Each schema is private. Changing it is a local decision. Cross-service data moves through APIs and
events, which are versioned and can evolve.

```text
order-service      ──▶  orders_db
inventory-service  ──▶  inventory_db
payment-service    ──▶  payments_db

cross-service reads → published API or replicated read model
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

The obvious objection is joins: the order list needs product names, which live in catalog. Three
answers, in order of preference.

The first one is worth pausing on because it is usually dismissed as denormalisation. It is not — it
is **temporal correctness**. An order placed at £9.99 should still say £9.99 after the price changes.
Fetching the current price would be actively wrong.

<Callout type="prod">
The anti-corruption layer is the one that repeatedly proves its worth. When you integrate with a
legacy system or a third-party API, translate their model into yours at the boundary — a thin
adapter package, and nothing beyond it knows their field names.

Without it, `SAP_CUST_REF_NO` propagates through your domain, and replacing that system becomes a
project that touches every service. With it, replacing them is one adapter.
</Callout>

## Sizing

There is no correct number of lines of code. The useful tests are about the team, not the code:

- **Can one team own it?** Not "could two people understand it" — could one team be responsible for
  it, on call for it, and plan its roadmap.
- **Can you explain what it does in one sentence** with no "and"? "It manages the order lifecycle" is
  a service. "It manages orders and sends notifications and calculates tax" is three.
- **Can you rewrite it in a couple of weeks?** Not that you would, but if the answer is "no, months",
  the service is doing too much to reason about as a unit.

Err towards **too large**. Merging two services is an afternoon; splitting one that has grown
entangled internals is a quarter. A boundary you can move later is worth more than one you got
theoretically right today.
