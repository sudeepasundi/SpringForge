Someone joins the team and asks why orders and payments are separate services. Three people give
three different answers, none confident, and the one who made the decision left last year.

That is not a documentation problem. It is that the decision was never written down as a decision —
with the alternatives, the constraints at the time, and what was being traded away.

## An architecture decision record

An ADR is one short markdown file per decision, committed alongside the code, numbered and immutable.

```markdown title="docs/adr/0007-separate-payment-service.md"
# 7. Extract payment into its own service

Date: 2026-03-14
Status: Accepted
Deciders: platform team, payments team, security

## Context

Payment handling is currently inside the order monolith. Two constraints
have changed:

- We are pursuing PCI DSS certification. With payment code in the
  monolith, the entire monolith is in scope: every deploy needs
  additional review, and every engineer needs additional clearance.
- The payments team has grown to five people and is blocked on the
  monolith's shared release train, which ships twice a week.

Current volume is ~40 payments/minute at peak. Latency budget for
authorisation is 3s, of which the provider uses up to 2s.

## Decision

Extract payment into a separate service with its own database,
communicating with order-service via Kafka events.

## Consequences

### What this gives us
- PCI scope is limited to one service and one database.
- The payments team deploys independently.
- A payment outage degrades checkout rather than stopping it: orders
  are accepted as PENDING and the saga cancels them if payment fails.

### What it costs
- Placing an order is no longer one transaction. Checkout becomes a
  saga with compensation (see ADR 0008).
- Two more services to operate, plus the Kafka topics between them.
- A refund is now a compensating action rather than a rollback, which
  the finance team must be able to explain to customers.

### What we are betting on
- That payment volume grows enough to justify the operational cost.
  If volume stays flat, this was not worth it.

## Alternatives considered

**Keep payment in the monolith, scope PCI to the whole thing.**
Rejected: the audit cost applies to every deploy of every feature,
which is a permanent tax on unrelated work.

**Extract payment but share the database.**
Rejected: PCI scope follows the data, so this achieves nothing on the
primary constraint while adding network calls.

**Use a hosted checkout so card data never touches us.**
Rejected for now — it removes PCI scope entirely, which is genuinely
attractive, but the product team requires a checkout flow the hosted
version cannot express. Revisit if that requirement changes.
```

Five properties make that useful rather than ceremonial:

- **The context is dated and specific.** "40 payments/minute" lets a future reader ask whether the
  premise still holds.
- **The consequences include costs**, not only benefits. A decision record listing only upsides is
  advocacy.
- **The bet is named.** "If volume stays flat, this was not worth it" is falsifiable, and it tells a
  successor what to check.
- **Alternatives include why they were rejected**, which is the part that stops the same debate
  recurring.
- **The last alternative was rejected conditionally**, with the condition stated. That is the most
  valuable line in the document.

<Callout type="tip" title="Never edit an accepted ADR">
When a decision changes, write a new one and mark the old **Superseded by ADR 0019**. The value is in
the record of what was believed *at the time*, and editing destroys exactly that.

Numbered, immutable, append-only. The same discipline as a database migration, for the same reason.
</Callout>

## When to write one

Not for everything. The test: **would a competent engineer joining in two years be surprised, and
have no way to find out why?**

## Structuring a trade-off

Arguments about architecture usually stall because people are optimising for different qualities
without saying so. Naming the quality attributes converts an argument about preferences into a
comparison.

The weight column is what makes this a decision rather than a list. Two options can each win on
several rows; what settles it is which rows matter for **this** system, and saying so explicitly is
usually the moment agreement appears.

<Callout type="prod">
Write down the **numbers you assumed**. "We expect 500 orders/minute at peak and 2 million products"
lets someone in two years check whether the premise still holds.

Most architectures do not become wrong because the reasoning was bad. They become wrong because the
inputs changed and nobody noticed, since nobody recorded what the inputs were.
</Callout>

## Defending a design

Being questioned about a design is not an attack, and treating it as one is the most common way
engineers lose an argument they were right about.

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Defending the conclusion">

None of these engage with the question. The first two are appeals to authority, and the third
concedes there was no reasoning to examine.

```text
"That's just how it's done."

"Netflix does it this way."

"We already decided this."

"It's a best practice."
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="Defending the reasoning">

Each of these invites the questioner into the reasoning, which is where you find out whether it
holds.

```text
"We chose it because the payments team was blocked on the shared
 release train. If that constraint went away, so does the reason."

"We assumed 500 orders a minute at peak. At 50, this is
 over-engineered — is that number wrong?"

"The alternative we rejected was a shared library. The blocker was
 that every consumer has to redeploy. Does that matter less than
 we thought?"

"I don't know. Let me find out."
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

The last line matters most. "I don't know" is a complete and respectable answer, and a design
defended with confident guesses is one nobody can trust when it counts.

<Callout type="tip" title="The strongest position is knowing your own weakest point">
Every design has one. Naming it before anyone else does — "the weak point is that a Kafka outage
stops all order processing; we accepted that because our broker SLA is higher than our service SLO"
— demonstrates you have actually thought it through.

It also moves the conversation to whether that trade is right, which is the conversation worth
having.
</Callout>

## Keeping records alive

An ADR directory nobody reads is documentation debt with extra steps. Three cheap habits:

- **Link to ADRs from the code.** A comment reading `// See ADR 0007: payment is a separate service
  for PCI scope` puts the reasoning where someone will actually meet it.
- **Review them when the premise changes.** A quarterly pass asking "which of these assumed something
  that is no longer true?" is usually a thirty-minute meeting that finds one or two.
- **Write the ADR before the work, not after.** Writing it first is when it can still change the
  decision; writing it afterwards is minutes of a meeting that already happened.
