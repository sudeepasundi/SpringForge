You have circuit breakers, retries, bulkheads and fallbacks. You believe the system degrades
gracefully when payment-service goes down.

Do you know that, or do you believe it? The only way to know is to make payment-service go down while
you are watching.

## Chaos engineering is an experiment

The word "chaos" is misleading. This is not randomly breaking things — it is a controlled experiment
with a hypothesis, a blast radius and an abort condition.

<Steps>
  <Step title="State a hypothesis you could be wrong about">

Not "the system is resilient" but something falsifiable:

> When payment-service returns 100% errors, checkout continues to accept orders in PENDING, the
> circuit breaker opens within 30 seconds, the saga cancels them cleanly, and no order is left
> without a terminal state.

Every clause is checkable. If you cannot state one this specific, you do not yet know what you expect
— which is itself worth discovering.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Bound the blast radius">

Start in staging. Then production with a small share of traffic, at a low-traffic hour, with the team
watching and an abort ready.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Define the abort condition first">

"Error rate above 5%, or the on-call gets paged." Written down before you start, because deciding
mid-experiment is how a test becomes an incident.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Run it, and record what you actually saw">

Including the parts that worked. The value is as much in confirming the model as in finding the gap.

  </Step>
</Steps>

```yaml title="A LitmusChaos experiment: kill a payment-service pod"
apiVersion: litmuschaos.io/v1alpha1
kind: ChaosEngine
metadata:
  name: payment-pod-delete
spec:
  appinfo:
    appns: production
    applabel: "app=payment-service"
  chaosServiceAccount: litmus-admin
  experiments:
    - name: pod-delete
      spec:
        components:
          env:
            - { name: TOTAL_CHAOS_DURATION, value: "60" }
            - { name: PODS_AFFECTED_PERC, value: "33" }   # one of three
        probe:
          # The abort condition, enforced by the tooling rather than by a human
          # watching a dashboard.
          - name: checkout-still-works
            type: httpProbe
            mode: Continuous
            runProperties: { probeTimeout: 5, interval: 2, retry: 1 }
            httpProbe/inputs:
              url: https://api.acme.com/api/orders/health
              method: { get: { criteria: "==", responseCode: "200" } }
```

**Latency injection is the highest-value experiment**, because a slow dependency is more dangerous
than a dead one — the failure mode from
[module 08](/learn/resilience/failure-modes) — and it is the case least likely to have been tested.

```yaml title="Istio fault injection: 3 seconds of latency on half the traffic"
apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1beta1
kind: VirtualService
metadata:
  name: payment-service-chaos
spec:
  hosts: [payment-service]
  http:
    - fault:
        delay:
          percentage: { value: 50 }
          fixedDelay: 3s
      route:
        - destination: { host: payment-service }
```

<Callout type="prod">
Run the first experiments in staging, and understand what staging cannot tell you: it has less data,
fewer instances, no real traffic pattern and different timing. A cascade that needs 200 concurrent
requests to manifest will not appear with 5.

Staging tells you the mechanism works. Only production tells you it works at your scale — which is
why the progression is staging, then production at low traffic with an abort ready.
</Callout>

## Game days

The most valuable version needs no tooling: gather the team, break something in staging, and have
whoever is least familiar with the system debug it.

What that surfaces is rarely technical:

- The runbook references a dashboard that was renamed.
- Nobody knows who owns the payment integration since the reorganisation.
- The alert fires into a channel nobody watches.
- The rollback command in the wiki uses a deprecated flag.
- Two people assume the other is escalating.

None of that appears in an automated experiment, and all of it costs time during a real incident.

## Capacity planning

Two numbers, and neither is a guess if you have load tested.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find the capacity of one instance">

Load test to failure and record where p99 breaches the SLO — not where it crashes. If one instance
serves 250 requests per second within SLO, that is the number.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Work out how many you need">

```text
peak traffic       = 4,000 req/s
per instance       = 250 req/s within SLO
required           = 16 instances
+ headroom (40%)   = 23
+ N+1 for a zone   = 24
```

  </Step>
</Steps>

Headroom is not waste, and it is worth being explicit about what it buys:

<DecisionTable
  columns={['Covers']}
  rows={[

  ]}
/>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Autoscaling is not capacity planning">
An HPA reacts, and reaction takes time: metric collection, the scaling decision, pod scheduling,
image pull, JVM start, JIT warm-up. That is often two to three minutes for a JVM service.

A traffic spike that arrives in 20 seconds is served entirely by the capacity you already had. Set
`minReplicas` to handle your normal peak and use the HPA for genuine growth, not as a substitute for
provisioning.
</Callout>

## Cost

Cloud spend is an architectural output, not an accounting problem. The largest costs usually come
from decisions nobody framed as expensive.

The last row is why cost belongs in this module rather than in a finance conversation. A design that
makes six network calls per request costs more in compute, more in cross-zone transfer, more in
observability volume and more in engineer time — and by the time it shows up on a bill, it is the
expensive thing to change.

<Callout type="prod">
Put cost on a dashboard next to latency and error rate, broken down by service. A team that can see
their service costs £4,000 a month will find savings without being asked; a team that has never seen
the number cannot act on it.

The single highest-yield check is the ratio of requested to used CPU and memory. A 3:1 ratio across a
fleet means two thirds of the bill is buying nothing.
</Callout>
