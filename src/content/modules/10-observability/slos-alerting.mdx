An alert that fires and requires no action is worse than no alert. It consumes attention, and — far
worse — it teaches the team that alerts can be ignored. That habit is what makes the real page get
missed.

Service level objectives give you a principled way to decide what is worth waking someone for.

## SLI, SLO, SLA

<DecisionTable
  columns={['What it is', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    {

      cells: ['A measurement of one aspect of service quality', 'Fraction of requests served in under 300ms'],
    },
    {
      label: 'SLO',
      verdict: 'good',
      cells: ['A target for an SLI, over a window', '99.5% of requests under 300ms over 30 days'],
    },
    {
      label: 'SLA',
      cells: ['A contractual promise, with consequences', '99% availability, or the customer gets credit'],
    },
  ]}
/>

The SLO is the useful one internally. It converts "is the service healthy?" — which has no answer —
into a number you can measure, alert on, and make decisions with.

**Set the SLO below the SLA.** If you promise 99% contractually and target 99% internally, you are
paying a penalty the moment you miss. Targeting 99.5% gives you a margin to react.

## A good SLI measures what the user experiences

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Measuring what is easy">

None of these tell you whether anyone could use the service. CPU can be low because nothing is
working. Uptime counts a process that returns 500 to everything. Averages hide the tail entirely.

```promql
# The process is running — which says nothing about whether it serves anyone.
up{job="order-service"}

# Resource usage, which is a cause, not a symptom.
avg(rate(process_cpu_seconds_total[5m]))

# A mean, which is healthy while 1% of users cannot use the product.
rate(http_server_requests_seconds_sum[5m])
  / rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count[5m])
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="Measuring the user's experience">

A ratio of good events to total events, from the user's point of view, on the endpoints they
actually depend on.

```promql
# Availability: the fraction of requests that did not fail.
# 5xx only — a 400 means the client sent something wrong, which is not our outage.
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{status!~"5..", uri!="/actuator/**"}[5m]))
  / sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{uri!="/actuator/**"}[5m]))

# Latency: the fraction of requests served within the threshold.
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_bucket{le="0.3"}[5m]))
  / sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count[5m]))
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

<Callout type="tip" title="Exclude what the user does not experience">
Health checks, actuator endpoints and synthetic probes inflate your success ratio — they always
succeed and they are high volume, so they mask real failures.

Equally, exclude 4xx from the availability SLI. A client sending a malformed request is not your
service failing, and counting it means your SLO degrades when someone else ships a bug.
</Callout>

## Error budgets

An SLO of 99.5% over 30 days permits 0.5% of requests to fail. That is your **error budget**, and
framing it as a budget changes the conversation productively.

<DecisionTable
  columns={['Permitted downtime per 30 days', 'What it implies']}
  rows={[
    { label: '99%', cells: ['7h 12m', 'Comfortable; a single bad deploy is survivable'] },
    { label: '99.5%', verdict: 'good', cells: ['3h 36m', 'A reasonable default for an internal service'] },
    { label: '99.9%', verdict: 'mixed', cells: ['43m', 'Requires real investment in automation and redundancy'] },
    { label: '99.99%', verdict: 'bad', cells: ['4m 19s', 'Multi-region, and a rollback that is faster than a human'] },
  ]}
/>

Two operational rules follow, and they are the point of the exercise:

- **Budget remaining → ship.** Unused budget is a signal you could be moving faster or targeting
  reliability you do not need.
- **Budget exhausted → stop shipping features and fix reliability.** Not as a punishment, but because
  the number says the current failure rate is above what you agreed is acceptable.

That second rule is what makes an SLO more than a dashboard: it is a pre-agreed decision about how to
resolve the tension between shipping and stability, made before anyone is under pressure.

<Callout type="prod">
Do not set an SLO of 100%. It is unachievable, and pursuing it means never shipping — every change
is a risk to a target with no margin.

More usefully: pick the number from what users actually need. If your customers are batch systems
that retry overnight, 99% may be generous. If you are on the checkout path, 99.9% may be the floor.
The SLO is a product decision, not an engineering preference.
</Callout>

## Burn rate alerting

Alerting on "the SLO is breached" is too late — the budget is already gone. Alert instead on **how
fast you are consuming it**.

Burn rate is the multiple of the sustainable consumption rate. A burn rate of 1 exhausts the budget
exactly at the end of the window; a burn rate of 14.4 exhausts a 30-day budget in about two days.

<Mermaid
  caption="Two windows per alert. The long window has the sensitivity; the short one confirms the problem is still happening."

```yaml title="prometheus-rules.yaml"
groups:
  - name: order-service-slo
    rules:
      - alert: OrderServiceFastBurn
        # The short window stops the alert firing on a spike that has already
        # stopped — the long window alone would keep it lit for an hour.
        expr: |
          (
            slo:error_ratio:rate1h{service="order-service"} > (14.4 * 0.005)
            and
            slo:error_ratio:rate5m{service="order-service"} > (14.4 * 0.005)
          )
        labels:
          severity: page
        annotations:
          summary: "order-service is burning its error budget 14.4x too fast"
          runbook: "https://runbooks.acme.internal/order-service/high-error-rate"

      - alert: OrderServiceSlowBurn
        expr: |
          (
            slo:error_ratio:rate6h{service="order-service"} > (6 * 0.005)
            and
            slo:error_ratio:rate30m{service="order-service"} > (6 * 0.005)
          )
        labels:
          severity: ticket
        annotations:
          summary: "order-service will exhaust its error budget in under 5 days"
          runbook: "https://runbooks.acme.internal/order-service/elevated-errors"
```

The paired-window structure is the part worth copying. A single long window is slow to fire and slow
to clear; adding a short confirmation window makes the alert both responsive and self-resolving.

## Symptoms, not causes

<Compare>
  <Bad title="Alerting on causes">

Each of these can be true while the service is fine, and false while it is broken. High CPU on a
service that is comfortably serving traffic is not a problem. A restarted pod during a normal deploy
is not a problem. Together they produce a stream of pages nobody reads.

```yaml
- alert: HighCpu
  expr: rate(process_cpu_seconds_total[5m]) > 0.8

- alert: PodRestarted
  expr: increase(kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total[15m]) > 0

- alert: DiskUsageHigh
  expr: node_filesystem_avail_bytes / node_filesystem_size_bytes < 0.2
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="Alerting on symptoms, with causes as context">

One page, for the thing the user experiences. The cause metrics stay on the dashboard, where they
answer "why" once you are already looking.

```yaml
- alert: OrderServiceFastBurn
  expr: slo:error_ratio:rate1h > (14.4 * 0.005)
       and slo:error_ratio:rate5m > (14.4 * 0.005)
  labels: { severity: page }

# The exception: a cause that reliably predicts a symptom nobody can react to
# in time. A disk filling up gives hours of warning and then fails hard.
- alert: DiskWillFillIn4Hours
  expr: predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes[6h], 4 * 3600) < 0
  labels: { severity: ticket }
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

The exception in that second panel is worth noting: cause-based alerting is justified when the cause
gives you warning that the symptom will not. A disk filling is the canonical example — by the time
users notice, you have no time to act.

## Every page needs a runbook

An alert that wakes someone at 3am and gives them no starting point is an incomplete alert.

```markdown title="runbooks/order-service/high-error-rate.md"
# order-service: high error rate

## What this means
Requests to order-service are failing above the rate our SLO permits.
Users are seeing checkout failures.

## First checks, in order
1. Grafana → order-service → RED. Which endpoint, and what status codes?
2. Is the error 5xx from us, or a downstream propagating? Check
   `resilience4j_circuitbreaker_state` — an open breaker names the culprit.
3. Recent deploys: `kubectl rollout history deploy/order-service`.
4. Pick a failing trace from the exemplar link on the latency panel.

## Most common causes
- **A bad deploy.** Check whether the error rate starts at a rollout.
  Fix: `kubectl rollout undo deploy/order-service`.
- **payment-service degraded.** The breaker will be open. Their on-call
  is #payments-oncall. We degrade rather than fail — confirm the saga
  is cancelling orders cleanly rather than leaving them PENDING.
- **Database connection pool exhausted.** `hikaricp_connections_pending`
  above zero. Usually a slow query introduced by a recent change.

## If you need to buy time
Scaling out does not help if the database is the constraint. It does help
if the pool is per-instance and CPU is the limit.

## Escalation
Unresolved after 30 minutes → page the service owner (#shopflow-eng).
```

<Callout type="prod">
Review alerts monthly and delete the ones nobody acted on. An alert that fired eleven times and
produced no action is not a warning, it is noise — and its real cost is not the eleven interruptions
but the habit it builds of dismissing pages without reading them.

The metric to watch is the ratio of pages to incidents. If most pages are not incidents, the alerts
are wrong, not the responders.
</Callout>
