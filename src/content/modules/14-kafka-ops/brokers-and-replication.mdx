[Module 09](/learn/event-driven/messaging-foundations) told you to set `acks=all` for business
events. That advice was correct and incomplete: `acks=all` on its own can be satisfied by a single
broker, and if that broker then fails you lose the record you were told was safely written.

This lesson is the cluster underneath the client.

## What a cluster is

A Kafka cluster is a set of brokers. Each partition has one **leader** and some **followers**, and
they are spread across brokers so no single failure takes a partition offline.

Two facts that surprise people:

- **Followers do not serve reads.** Every produce and every consume for a partition goes to its
  leader. Replicas are for durability and failover, not for spreading read load.
- **Replication factor is per topic, not per cluster.** A three-broker cluster can hold topics with
  replication factor 1, 2 or 3, and a topic created with the wrong one is a topic that loses data.

## The in-sync replica set

The **ISR** is the subset of replicas currently caught up with the leader. A follower that falls
behind by more than `replica.lag.time.max.ms` (default 30s) is removed from it, and rejoins when it
catches up.

This is the concept the whole durability story rests on, because **`acks=all` means "all in-sync
replicas", not "all replicas".**

<Compare>
  <Bad title="acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1">

Two followers fall behind and drop out of the ISR. The ISR is now just the leader — so `acks=all` is
satisfied by the leader alone, and the producer is told the write succeeded.

The leader then fails. The record was never on another broker. It is gone, and the producer has an
acknowledgement saying otherwise.

```properties
# server.properties
min.insync.replicas=1        # the default, and the trap

# producer
acks=all                     # feels safe, guarantees nothing here
```

  </Bad>
  <Good title="acks=all with min.insync.replicas=2">

Now the leader refuses to accept a write unless at least two replicas are in sync. If the ISR shrinks
to one, produces fail with `NotEnoughReplicasException` — the producer finds out immediately rather
than being told a lie.

```properties
# Topic-level, so it survives a broker's own configuration being wrong.
min.insync.replicas=2
replication.factor=3

# producer
acks=all
enable.idempotence=true
```

  </Good>
</Compare>

<Callout type="prod">
`min.insync.replicas=2` with `replication.factor=3` is the standard durable configuration. It
tolerates losing one broker and keeps accepting writes; losing two stops writes rather than losing
data, which is the correct trade for anything financial.

Set it **on the topic**, not only in `server.properties`. A broker-level default does not apply to
topics that were created with an explicit value, and topics are frequently created by someone else's
Terraform.
</Callout>

## Unclean leader election

The other setting that quietly decides whether you lose data.

When every in-sync replica is unavailable, Kafka has two options: wait for one to come back
(unavailable but correct), or promote an out-of-sync replica (available but missing records).

```properties
# The default since Kafka 3.0, and the right one for business data.
unclean.leader.election.enable=false
```

With it `false`, a partition with no in-sync replica simply stops serving until one returns. That is
an availability incident, and it is preferable to silently truncating committed records — the
consumers that already read them will never be told those records are gone.

Turn it on only for topics where losing data is genuinely acceptable: metrics, clickstream, anything
you would resample rather than reconstruct.

## KRaft

Kafka used to depend on ZooKeeper for cluster metadata. **KRaft** replaces it with a Raft quorum
inside Kafka itself, and it is the only supported mode in current versions.

```properties title="A KRaft broker that is also a controller — fine for small clusters"
process.roles=broker,controller
node.id=1
controller.quorum.voters=1@kafka-1:9093,2@kafka-2:9093,3@kafka-3:9093

listeners=PLAINTEXT://:9092,CONTROLLER://:9093
controller.listener.names=CONTROLLER
inter.broker.listener.name=PLAINTEXT
```

<Callout type="tip" title="Separate the roles once the cluster matters">
Combined `broker,controller` nodes are fine for development and small clusters. At scale, run
dedicated controllers — three of them — so that a broker under heavy produce load cannot starve the
controller and destabilise cluster metadata for everyone.

The quorum needs an odd number and tolerates `(n-1)/2` failures: three controllers survive one
failure, five survive two.
</Callout>

## Rack awareness

Replicas on three brokers in the same availability zone survive a broker failure and not a zone
failure. `broker.rack` tells Kafka to spread replicas across zones:

```properties
broker.rack=eu-west-1a
```

With it set on every broker, replica assignment distributes each partition's replicas across
distinct racks automatically. Without it, a three-replica partition can land entirely in one zone,
and you discover that during the zone outage.

The cost is cross-zone replication traffic, which most cloud providers bill for. That is the trade:
pay continuously for replication, or lose the partition when a zone goes.

## Is Kafka even the right broker?

Worth asking, because a great deal of Kafka is used where a queue would do.

**Choose Kafka when you need replay, several independent consumers of the same stream, or ordering
by key at volume.** Choose a queue when you need per-message acknowledgement, routing, delays, or
simply have a work queue with one consumer — which is most task-processing.

Using Kafka as a task queue is the common mistake: the positional-offset model means one poison
message blocks its whole partition, and you end up rebuilding per-message acknowledgement badly.
